Limitation

⚠ 4 caveats
⚠ Comment ≠ Commenter

Language ≠ Diagnosis

Depression-indicative language does not imply the commenter has depression.

⚠ Sentiment ≠ Depression

Necessary but not sufficient

Negative sentiment alone is not enough — the three-layer method addresses this; false positives still possible.

⚠ Top-Comment Bias

Loud voices dominate

Sorting by likes favors emotionally resonant language; quieter expressions of distress may be underrepresented.

⚠ No Clinical Ground Truth

Linguistic proxies

All labels are linguistic proxies, not clinical assessments. Results measure depression-indicative pattern prevalence in public discourse — not diagnostic prevalence.

Recommendation

→ 3 audiences
1For Research Peers

Methodology change.

For NLP / mental-health researchers measuring online discourse
  • When measuring depression signals in online discourse, always include a first-person syntactic filter. Without it, narrative-template categories — fitness, recovery vlogs — get inflated by the c3 phenomenon.
2For Clinical / Public Health

Treat comments as a signal source.

For public-health monitoring teams and platform-policy stakeholders
  • Top-comment bias is a feature and a bug. Platforms boost emotionally-resonant comments to the top — so users seeking peer stories find them faster, but our 21% number is an upper bound for top-ranked comments, not the full population. Ask of platforms: offer researchers stratified sampling APIs across the comment-rank distribution.
3For Our Own Future Work

Three concrete next steps.

What we owe ourselves before claiming generality
  • Cross-lingual extension. The pipeline is English-only (LIWC + spaCy English models). Chinese, Spanish, and other languages would need a re-built Layer 2 lexicon and Layer 3 subject-detection rules.
  • Add a Layer 4 narrative-template classifier. Train explicitly on c3-style weight-loss and recovery-arc structures so the pipeline can flag them out instead of mis-firing on them.
