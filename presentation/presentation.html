01
Background & Motivation

Why measure depression language in general-interest videos?

— THE GAP IN PRIOR WORK

Most studies sample from communities where users have already self-identified as depressed.

Photo · Unsplash
The Problem

Self-selection biases what we see.

Most prior work collects data from depression-specific communities like r/depression. Users there have already sought out a space to express distress.

That's a clean signal — but it doesn't tell us how depression-related expression surfaces in ordinary public discourse, where people don't go looking to be heard.

FIELD NOTE
"

I've been struggling so much lately, this is the only thing keeping me going.

— Beneath a music video, found in pilot data
Research Questions
  • Q1

    Across YouTube content categories, how does the natural prevalence of depression-indicative language vary?

  • Q2

    To what extent does video-level contextual framing shape the linguistic patterns of depression-related self-disclosure?

The unit of analysis is the comment, not the video.

A music video has no depression relevance. But a comment underneath it — "I've been feeling so empty lately" — does. We sample from mainstream content across five categories, then scan every comment with a three-layer detector. This better reflects the true prevalence of depression-related expression in everyday online discourse, and lets us compare how that expression differs across content contexts.

STEP 00

Data Collection Strategy

collect.py

Sampling Logic

  • top-NWithin each category, rank videos by view count and select top N.
  • top-50Per video: 50 most-liked comments, with video_id as foreign key.
  • no filterNo depression keywords used — relevance determined post-hoc at the comment level.

Five Categories

  • baselineMental Health — expected higher depression signal rate
  • adjacentFitness / Wellness — adjacent to mental health; motivational framing
  • mainstreamMusic / Entertainment — high-traffic mainstream content; general audience
  • chargedNews / Current Events — emotionally charged but non-personal framing
  • narrativePersonal Vlog — first-person narrative; higher self-disclosure potential

Target ≈ 40–50 videos per category · ~250 total · ~12.5K comments (top-50 by likes per video)

STEP 02

Three-Layer Detection

comment_analysis.py

Sentiment alone is not enough — "this video is garbage" is negative but not depression-indicative. Three layers of increasing specificity:

Layer 1 · Broad Signal
Multi-tool sentiment scoring
Each comment scored independently by VADER (compound), TextBlob (polarity + subjectivity), and AFINN (word-level valence sum) — three tools for a more robust signal than any single model.
Layer 2 · Domain-Specific
TF-IDF weighted lexicon matching
Match against LIWC categories (negative emotion, sadness, anxiety, health) + a depression-specific wordlist. TF-IDF weighting with locked vocabulary captures how prominently depression-relevant terms appear, extended to bigrams ("feel hopeless", "can't sleep").
Layer 3 · High-Precision
Self-disclosure pattern detection
spaCy POS tagging identifies first-person pronoun + depression-vocabulary combinations ("I feel hopeless", "I've been struggling"). Captures genuine personal disclosure rather than reactions to video content. Strongest research signal.

Aggregate Output

  • compositePer-comment signal score combining all 3 layers

Auxiliary Features

  • lex_diversityDepressive language tends to be repetitive
  • word_countFilter very short comments (<5 words)

05
Limitations

What we are not claiming.

⚠ Comment ≠ Commenter

Language ≠ Diagnosis

Depression-indicative language does not imply the commenter has depression.

⚠ Sentiment ≠ Depression

Necessary but not sufficient

Negative sentiment alone is not enough — three-layer method addresses this; false positives still possible.

⚠ Top-Comment Bias

Loud voices dominate

Sorting by likes favors emotionally resonant language; quieter expressions of distress may be underrepresented.

⚠ No Clinical Ground Truth

Linguistic proxies

All labels are linguistic proxies, not clinical assessments. Results measure depression-indicative pattern prevalence in public discourse — not diagnostic prevalence.
